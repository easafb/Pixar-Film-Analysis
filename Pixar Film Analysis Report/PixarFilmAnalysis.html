1. Introduction

In this project, we analyze the Pixar Film Ratings dataset to understand the relationship between critical acclaim and commercial success. Our objective is to explore how audience perceptions and critic scores have evolved over the years and their impact on the studio’s financial performance.

This report covers the first stage of our project: Data Cleaning and Preparation.

2. Data Cleaning and Preparation

2.1. Loading and Merging the Datasets

In this step, we load the three main tables from the pixarfilms package: pixar_films, pixar_ratings, and pixar_box_office. To conduct a comprehensive analysis, we merge these datasets into a single “Master Dataset” using the common film column as our primary key.

Before merging, we inspect each dataset individually. pixar_films contains 27 films with basic information: film number, name, release date, runtime, and rating. public_response contains 24 films with critic scores from Rotten Tomatoes, Metacritic, CinemaScore, and Critics’ Choice. box_office also contains 24 films with budget and revenue data.

We noticed that both public_response and box_office have only 24 rows compared to 27 in pixar_films — this means 3 films do not have scores or financial data yet, likely the most recent unreleased ones.

We also notice that number is stored as text (chr) in pixar_films and will need to be fixed after merging. We use film as the join key since it appears in all three tables.

2.2.Handling Missing Values and Column Refinement

Real-world datasets often contain “data quality problems” such as missing values (NA) or redundant columns.

In this step we prepare the master dataset for analysis. First, we fix two data type issues identified during inspection: the number column is converted from text to integer using as.integer(), and the value "N/A" in film_rating is converted to a real missing value using na_if().

We then check for duplicate films using count() — no duplicates were found, confirming each film appears exactly once.

Next, we remove the 3 unreleased films using drop_na(rotten_tomatoes) since they have no critic scores or box office data, making them unsuitable for analysis.

Finally, we rename film to movie_title for clarity. The missing value audit confirms that only cinema_score (1 missing) and critics_choice (2 missing) still contain NA values — these are kept intentionally as they represent early films that were not reviewed by those sources.

# Fix number column from text to integer
# Fix "N/A" in film_rating to real NA
pixar_master <- pixar_master %>%
  mutate(
    number = as.integer(number),
    film_rating = na_if(film_rating, "N/A")
  )

2.3. Reasoning and Data Quality Check

As part of our quality checklist, we summarize the key decisions made during the cleaning process:

ID Uniqueness:We verified using count() that each film appears exactly once in the dataset. No duplicate rows were found.

Missing Observations: We removed 3 unreleased films using drop_na(rotten_tomatoes) since they had no critic scores or box office data. The remaining missing values in cinema_score and critics_choice were kept intentionally — they represent early films not covered by those sources, and removing them would mean losing valuable box office and rating data for well-known films.

3.2 New Variables and trends

We create three foundational variables. release_year extracts the year from release_date using year(), giving us a numeric variable we can use for trend analysis. decadeuses case_when() to group films into their respective decades, allowing us to compare Pixar’s output across time periods. Finally, profit is calculated as the difference between box_office_worldwide and budget, giving us a direct measure of each film’s financial return.

pixar_eda <- pixar_clean %>%
  mutate(
    
    #1. release year from date
    release_year = as.integer(year(release_date)),
    
    #2. Decade the film was released in
    decade = case_when(
      release_year < 2000 ~ "1990s",
      release_year < 2010 ~ "2000s",
      release_year < 2020 ~ "2010s",
      TRUE ~ "2020s"
    ),
    
    #3. profit
    profit = box_office_worldwide - budget
  )

Next, we create three variables related to financial performance and critical reception. roi measures the return on investment as a percentage, calculated by dividing profit by budget and multiplying by 100 — for example, Toy Story achieved an ROI of over 1100%, reflecting its enormous commercial success relative to its budget. avg_critic_score takes the average of rotten_tomatoes and metacritic to create a single combined critic score for each film. Finally, commercial_success uses if_else() to create a simple yes/no flag indicating whether a film made a profit.

pixar_eda <- pixar_eda %>%
  mutate(
    # 4. Return on investment as percentage
    roi = ((box_office_worldwide - budget) / budget) * 100,
    
    # 5. Average critic score
    avg_critic_score = (rotten_tomatoes + metacritic) / 2,
    
    # 6. Was the film profitable
    commercial_success = if_else(profit > 0, "Yes", "No")
  )

In the final group of new variables, we create four categorical columns. era uses case_when() to divide Pixar’s history into three periods — Early Pixar (up to 2005), Golden Age (2006 to 2012), and Modern Pixar (2013 onwards) — reflecting distinct phases in the studio’s creative and commercial development. runtime_category classifies each film as Short (under 95 minutes), Medium (95 to 110 minutes), or Long (over 110 minutes). is_sequel is to flag films whose titles contain numbers or roman numerals, identifying sequels in the franchise. Finally, budget_category groups films into Low, Medium, and High budget tiers based on their production budget.

pixar_eda <- pixar_eda %>%
  mutate(
    # 7. era of Pixar filmmaking
    era = case_when(
      release_year <= 2005 ~ "Early Pixar",
      release_year <= 2012 ~ "Golden Age",
      TRUE ~ "Modern Pixar"
    ),
    
    # 8. Runtime category
    runtime_category = case_when(
      run_time < 95 ~ "Short",
      run_time <= 110 ~ "Medium",
      TRUE ~ "Long"
    ),
    
    # 9. sequel or not
    is_sequel = if_else(
      str_detect(movie_title, "2|3|4|II|III"), "Yes", "No"
    ),
    
    # 10. Budget category
    budget_category = case_when(
      budget < 100000000 ~ "Low",
      budget <= 175000000 ~ "Medium",
      TRUE ~ "High"
    )
  )

4. Modeling

In this section we build two statistical models to answer specific analytical questions about Pixar films.

Question 1: Can we predict a Pixar film’s worldwide box office revenue based on its budget and critic scores? We will use linear regression to answer this question, as we are predicting a continuous numeric outcome.

Question 2: Can we predict whether a Pixar film will be commercially successful based on its budget, critic scores and runtime? We will use logistic regression to answer this question, as the outcome is binary — a film either made a profit (Yes) or did not (No).

4.1 Linear Regression: Predicting Worldwide Box Office Revenue

We use linear regression to predict box_office_worldwide based on budget, rotten_tomatoes, and metacritic. These predictors were chosen because our EDA showed a positive relationship between budget and box office revenue, and critic scores are central to our research question about critical acclaim and financial performance.

# Set seed

4.3 Model Comparison

We built two models to answer our analytical questions. Here we compare them across several dimensions.

Predicting ability: The linear regression model showed a reasonable fit on the training data (R-squared = 0.58) but performed poorly on the test set (R-squared = 0.065), with an average prediction error of $356 million. The logistic regression model achieved 83.3% accuracy but only by predicting “Yes” for every film, which is not genuinely useful.

Statistical significance: In the linear regression model, budget and metacritic were statistically significant predictors. In the logistic regression model, none of the predictors were statistically significant.

Limitations: Both models are heavily limited by the small dataset of only 23 films. With so few observations, it is difficult to build reliable models that generalize well to new data. The logistic regression was further limited by class imbalance — almost all films were commercially successful, leaving very little variation for the model to learn from.

Conclusion: Between the two models, linear regression performed better in terms of meaningful results. It identified budget and Metacritic score as significant predictors of box office revenue, which aligns with our EDA findings. The logistic regression was not able to produce useful predictions given the nature of the data.
